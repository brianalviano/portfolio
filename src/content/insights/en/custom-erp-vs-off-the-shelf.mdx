## Choose off-the-shelf when the workflow is standard

Ready-made software is usually the better first choice when the business process is common, the available product covers most requirements, and the team can adapt without creating operational risk.

It offers faster implementation, known pricing, documentation, and an existing support ecosystem.

## Choose custom software when the workflow is the advantage

Custom development becomes more reasonable when the workflow is genuinely specific, several systems must be connected, manual reconciliation creates repeated cost, or existing tools force the team into unreliable workarounds.

The strongest reason for custom software is not preference. It is measurable operational fit.

## Compare total cost, not only license cost

A subscription may look cheaper than development, but the comparison should include migration, customization, duplicated data entry, integration tools, process changes, training, and the cost of limitations over time.

Custom software also has continuing costs: maintenance, hosting, security updates, monitoring, and future improvements.

## Evaluate ownership and dependency

With SaaS, the vendor controls the roadmap, pricing model, and sometimes data export options. With custom software, the organization gains more control but also accepts responsibility for technical continuity.

Documentation, source-code ownership, deployment access, and handover plans should be clarified before development begins.

## Use a staged decision

A useful approach is to map the workflow, identify the highest-cost bottlenecks, evaluate available products, and prototype only the gaps that matter. A custom ERP does not have to replace everything at once.

The right solution may be off-the-shelf, custom, or a hybrid of both.
